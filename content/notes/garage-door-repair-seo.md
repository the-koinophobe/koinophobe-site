Most of the sites I work on are roofing, pool deck, lawn and shutter companies, and I don't have a garage door company among them. Garage door searches work under the same rules as those trades, so this is the setup I'd build, and any results I mention come from the trades I do work in.

## The urgent search

A garage door usually fails at a bad moment. The spring snaps with the car inside, the door comes off its track, or the opener stops responding on the way to work. The owner searches `garage door repair near me` or `broken garage door spring` on a phone, looks at the map, and calls one of the first few listings.

That search is won in two places. The first is the map pack, the three businesses Google shows next to a map. The second is the phone number on your site, which has to be one tap away on every page.

## The Business Profile

The map pack runs on your Google Business Profile. My note on [how the map pack ranks businesses](/notes/google-map-pack-ranking) covers how Google picks the three. The settings to get right first:

![A modern garage door with wood panels and a row of windows](/notes/photos/garage-door-repair-seo-1.webp "Photo: Ryan Waldman on Unsplash")

- **Primary category** that matches garage door work, with secondary categories only for work you do. [How to choose categories](/notes/google-business-profile-categories) covers the trade-offs.
- **Hours** set to when someone answers. Mark 24 hours only if a person picks up at night.
- **Service areas**, up to 20, set by city or ZIP within about 2 hours' drive. Remove the street address if customers don't visit you.
- **Name** exactly as it appears on your truck and invoices. Adding "Spring Repair 24/7" to it breaks Google's rules.
- **Photos** of your own trucks, crew and finished doors, added as jobs finish.

## Click-to-call on every page

Put a tap-to-call number (a `tel:` link) in the header of every page so it's the first thing visible on a phone. If you do same-day or after-hours repairs, say so near the top along with the towns you cover. Then tap it on your own phone at night and see who answers.

## Pages by job and by product

People search two ways: the problem and the product. Build pages for both.

| Page | What it should answer |
|---|---|
| Spring replacement | Torsion or extension springs, whether you replace them in pairs, how long it takes |
| Door off track | What causes it, why the owner shouldn't force it, same-day availability |
| Opener repair and installation | Brands you service and install, belt or chain drive, smart and battery backup models |
| Cable and roller repair | Signs of wear, what's included in a tune-up |
| New garage doors | Materials, insulated options, the brands you install, typical install time |

Each page needs a title tag naming the job and your main town, the tap-to-call button, your own job photos and the questions customers ask on the phone. Title tags decide whether a ranking turns into a click. Across three Florida sites I work on, 307 searches ranked in Google's top ten in one 28-day window and got zero clicks.

## Say what things cost

The person standing in front of a broken door wants to know what the repair will cost before they call. If you can publish a starting price or a range for spring replacement and other common repairs, put it on the page, along with what's included and whether there's a service call fee. If prices vary too much to publish, explain what drives the price and say you'll quote before any work starts.

## Reviews, asked the right way

When three listings look alike, reviews are one of the few things that set them apart. Ask every customer, the same way, soon after the job. Google prohibits offering incentives for reviews and asking only the customers you think are happy (review gating). The FTC's fake reviews rule took effect October 21, 2024, with civil penalties up to $51,744 per violation. [Getting more Google reviews](/notes/google-reviews-for-roofers) covers the routine.

## A crowded, messy map pack

Garage door repair is one of the urgent trades where the map results can include listings that don't match a real local company. Some are lead generators that sell the call on. Some sit at a virtual office or a mailbox address. Some are duplicates of one business. [How to report fake listings](/notes/report-fake-google-business-listings) covers the evidence to gather and which of Google's forms to use.

Google's Business Profile guidelines cover these cases. A profile has to match the business as it appears on its signage and branding. A rented address the business doesn't operate from (a virtual office) isn't eligible, and neither is a remote mailbox. A business shouldn't have more than one profile per location.

If you see a listing that breaks these rules, use Suggest an edit on it in Google Maps, or file Google's business redressal complaint form. Don't answer with extra listings or a keyword-stuffed name of your own. That puts your real profile at risk, and my note on [suspended Business Profiles](/notes/google-business-profile-suspended) covers what happens if yours gets suspended.

## Town pages and tracking

A page for each town you want work in can help with `garage door repair [town]` searches, as long as each one has something true about that town. Near-identical pages with the town name swapped count as doorway spam under Google's policies.

Then track it. Make website phone taps a GA4 key event, count Business Profile calls separately, and test your forms every month. In July 2026 I found a client's form submissions had been dropping since March because of an email setting, and nobody noticed for four months, me included.
