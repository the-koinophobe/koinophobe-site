**The short answer:** buyers search for the inspection they need ("home inspection [town]", "sewer scope inspection", "radon testing"), check the price, look at a sample report, read reviews, and book. Give them a page for each inspection and add-on, publish prices by home size, show a real sample report, and let them book online. Even buyers an agent sends you will look you up first.

## Two kinds of customer, one website

Agent referrals still drive a lot of inspection work. But buyers now look up every name an agent gives them, and some skip the agent's list altogether. Both groups land on the same pages and ask the same questions: what's included, how much, how soon, and what will the report look like?

## The pages to build

**The main inspection types:**

- **Pre-purchase inspection.** Your core page. What you inspect, how long it takes, whether the buyer should attend, when the report arrives.
- **Pre-listing inspection,** for sellers who want no surprises.
- **New construction and phase inspections,** if you do them. Builders' buyers often don't realize they can hire their own.
- **Condo and townhome inspections,** if you price them differently.

**The add-ons, each with its own page,** because people search them by name:

- Radon testing
- Sewer scope
- Termite or wood-destroying insect inspection, where you're licensed for it
- Mold sampling, pool and spa, well and septic, as you offer them

Each add-on page: what it checks, why it matters in your area, how it's done, the price, and whether it can be booked with the main inspection.

## The sample report

The report is what the buyer is really paying for. Put a sample online, with personal details removed. Show how issues are flagged, how many photos there are, and how you explain what's urgent versus what's normal for the home's age. It's often the page that convinces someone choosing between you and two others.

## Prices and booking

Publish prices by square footage or home size, and add-on prices. Say what changes the price (age, crawl spaces, extra units). Buyers are on a deadline, often an inspection period of a week or so, so online booking that shows real availability is worth having.

## Licensing and credentials

Many states license home inspectors. If yours does, put the license number on the site. List any association membership and continuing education you keep up with, and your insurance. These are the trust details buyers and agents both check.

## The Business Profile

Primary category: **Home inspector**. Hide your home address if clients don't come to you and list your service areas. Post photos from inspections (attics, panels, roofs) and keep hours accurate. Ask every client for a review, and ask them to mention what stood out, like the report or how you walked them through it. [How to ask for Google reviews](/notes/how-to-ask-for-google-reviews) has the wording.

## Keep agents in the loop

Your website helps with agents too. Agents send buyers to inspectors who are easy to book, communicate well and write clear reports. A page for agents explaining your scheduling, report turnaround and how you talk about issues with buyers gives them something to share.

## Track bookings

Track calls, booking-form submissions and online bookings separately, so you can see whether the add-on pages pay for themselves. [GA4 form tracking](/notes/ga4-form-tracking-contractors) and [call tracking](/notes/call-tracking-for-contractors) cover the setup.
