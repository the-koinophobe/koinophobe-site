**The short answer:** foundation repair isn't an impulse search. A homeowner sees a crack, searches what it means, reads for days or weeks, and calls two or three companies. The site that answers the early questions honestly ("is this crack serious?") is the one they trust when it's time to book an inspection. So build pages for the warning signs, the repair methods, the cost factors and the inspection, and make booking the inspection easy.

## How the search unfolds

Most people don't start with "foundation repair near me". They start with what they see:

- "horizontal crack in basement wall"
- "doors sticking and cracks above door frame"
- "sloping floor in old house"
- "is a stair step crack in brick serious"

Then they move to "foundation repair cost", "piering vs mudjacking", "does homeowners insurance cover foundation repair". Only then "foundation repair [town]".

A site with only a services page and a contact form shows up at the end of that, if at all. The companies that get the call were there at the start.

## The warning-sign pages

Write one page per sign you see often, in your area's housing stock:

- Cracks in basement walls: vertical, horizontal and stair-step, and what each usually means.
- Doors and windows that stick or won't latch.
- Sloping or bouncy floors.
- Gaps between walls and ceiling, or around window frames.
- Water in the basement or crawl space, if you do that work too.

Be honest. Say when a crack is usually cosmetic and when it needs a look. A page that tells someone "this one's probably fine, here's how to monitor it" earns more trust than one that makes every hairline crack an emergency, and those people remember you when the next crack isn't fine.

Add your own photos from inspections. Photos of real cracks with a tape measure next to them are worth more than any stock image.

## The repair-method pages

One page per method you use: steel or helical piers, wall anchors or braces, crawl space supports, slab leveling. For each: what it fixes, how the job goes day by day, how long it takes, what the yard looks like after, and the warranty.

Homeowners compare methods between companies. Clear explanations help them understand why your quote recommends what it does.

## Cost, inspection and warranty

**Cost page.** Exact prices need an inspection, but you can explain what drives the number: method, number of piers or anchors, access, depth, soil, and whether there's water to deal with. If you can, give ranges from your own recent jobs. People researching for weeks want to know the scale.

**Inspection page.** Is it free? How long does it take? Do you provide a written report or an engineer's letter? Who comes out? Make booking it the main action on the site.

**Warranty page.** Many foundation warranties transfer when the house sells. If yours does, say so plainly; it matters to people planning to sell.

## Your region matters

Foundation problems depend on soil and climate: expansive clay, freeze and thaw, high water tables, older homes on rubble foundations. Write about what you actually see in your area. A note on why so many homes in your county have a particular problem is something no national lead site can copy, and it's what local town pages should be built on.

## The Business Profile

Use the most specific primary category that fits, often **Foundation contractor** or a close match in Google's list; check what the top map results in your area use. Post inspection and repair photos, and ask every customer for a review that mentions the repair. Reviews matter a lot when the ticket is this big.
