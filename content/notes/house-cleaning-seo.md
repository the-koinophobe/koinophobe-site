A cleaning company earns its steady money from recurring customers, but most people don't start by searching "biweekly cleaning service." They search for the job in front of them: `deep cleaning`, `move out cleaning`, `house cleaners near me`. The site has to catch that search, book the first clean, and then sell the schedule.

Cleaning isn't a trade I've worked in. Most of my sites are roofing, pool deck, lawn and shutter companies. The search side works the same way, though, so this is the setup I'd use, and the results I mention come from those other trades.

## A page for each kind of clean

Build a page for each service, written for the person who needs it:

- **Standard recurring cleaning.** What a regular visit covers, how often you can come (weekly, biweekly, monthly) and whether it's the same cleaner each time.
- **Deep cleaning.** What you do beyond a standard clean: baseboards, inside appliances, behind furniture. A room-by-room checklist answers the first question people ask.
- **Move-in and move-out cleaning.** Who books it (tenants trying to get a deposit back, landlords, sellers), what's included, and whether you work to a landlord's checklist.
- **Specialty work you take on.** Short-term rental turnovers, post-construction cleanup, offices. Only the ones you want more of.

Each page needs a title tag with the service and your main town, a booking button near the top, and the questions customers ask before they book. Then each page links to your recurring service, with a line about how a one-time clean turns into a schedule.

## A prices page

People search for cleaning prices before they call anyone. A page that answers with real numbers can rank for those searches, and it filters out people who were never going to book.

If you can't quote exact prices, give ranges by home size and type of clean, explain what moves the price up (pets, the home's condition, frequency), and say whether recurring visits cost less than one-time cleans. Prices change, so put the date you last updated the page on it.

## Trust is the deciding factor

You're asking people to let strangers into their home, often while they're out. Whatever you do to make that safe belongs on the site, on its own page and in a short block on every service page:

- How you hire and screen cleaners, including background checks if you run them.
- Insurance and bonding, if you carry them.
- What happens if something is damaged or the clean isn't right.
- Whether customers get the same cleaner each visit.

Only claim what's true and current, and update the page when your insurance renews.

## Business Profile settings

Choose **House cleaning service** as your primary category if you mainly clean homes, or **Commercial cleaning service** if you mainly clean offices. [How to choose categories](/notes/google-business-profile-categories) covers the rest.

If customers don't come to you, hide your address and set service areas, up to 20, within about two hours' drive. Service areas don't decide where you rank in the map pack. Your website's town pages do more for the towns farther out. [Service area businesses on Google](/notes/service-area-business-google-business-profile) explains how it works.

## Reviews within the rules

Recurring customers see you every week or two, which gives you more chances to ask for a review than most trades get. Ask every customer the same way, after a clean that went well for them, with a direct link to your review form. Google prohibits incentives for reviews, so no discount for a five-star rating, and the FTC's rule on fake reviews carries civil penalties per violation. [Getting more Google reviews](/notes/google-reviews-for-roofers) is written for roofers, and the method is the same.

## Track bookings

Make each booking a key event in GA4, whether it comes from a form, a booking tool or a phone tap, and test the booking form every month. In July 2026 I found a client's forms had been failing since March because of an email setting. Nobody noticed for four months. [The three events every local business should track](/notes/three-events-local-business) covers the setup.

Then watch which service pages lead to recurring sign-ups. That tells you which pages to improve first. Lawn care runs on the same one-time-to-recurring pattern, and my [lawn care SEO](/notes/lawn-care-seo) note shows a similar plan page.
