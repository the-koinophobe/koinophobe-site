"Who's a good family dentist near Westchase that takes Delta Dental?" "Do I need a lawyer for a small claims case in Ohio?" "Best accountant for a restaurant owner in Austin?"

People ask assistants these questions now, and the answer often names a few practices. If you run a dental office, a law firm, an accounting practice or a clinic, being one of those names is worth a lot, because the person asking is close to booking.

Here's what professional practices can do, built on what I've measured and what the platforms document.

## Why practices are a special case

**High trust, high stakes.** People choosing a dentist or a lawyer read carefully. Assistants seem to lean on the same kind of trust signals people do: credentials, reviews, how long you've practiced, whether others recommend you.

**Location matters.** Most practices serve a local area. Google's AI features draw on Business Profile data; Google tells site owners to keep it current.

**Rules apply.** Medical, dental and legal advertising is regulated. Whatever you publish for AI search has to follow the same rules as the rest of your marketing.

## What I'd do for a practice

### 1. Let the assistants read your site

Many practice websites run on templates from industry marketing vendors, and some block AI crawlers or load text with JavaScript. Check that OAI-SearchBot, Claude-SearchBot, PerplexityBot and Googlebot can reach your pages. The [free crawler check](/saas-seo#check) takes ten seconds. [What each crawler does](/notes/ai-crawlers-robots-txt).

### 2. Make your practice easy to identify

- **One name, phone and address** everywhere: site, Google Business Profile, Healthgrades, Avvo, Zocdoc, Yelp, your association's directory.
- **Each provider gets a bio page** with credentials, education, licenses, languages and what they treat or practice. Assistants answering "who" questions need a "who" to point at.
- **Services listed plainly.** "Root canals," "dental implants," "Invisalign," "estate planning," "small business tax returns." Every service a client might ask about should appear in words on your site.

### 3. Answer the questions people ask before booking

These are the pages that get cited, because they're what people ask:

- **Cost.** "How much does a crown cost without insurance in [city]?" Give a range and what changes it. Most practices don't, so the one that does gets quoted.
- **Insurance.** Which plans you accept, in text, not an image of logos.
- **What happens.** "What to expect at your first visit," "how long does a root canal take," "what to bring to a consultation."
- **Do I need you?** "Do I need a lawyer for...," "can I do my own business taxes," "when should I see a physical therapist instead of a doctor."

Write each one with a direct answer first, then the detail, reviewed by the professional who'd answer it in person. In [my study of AI citations](/notes/ai-cited-vs-ranked-page), assistants cited article-shaped pages and passed over pages that only described a business. [Content that AI cites](/notes/content-ai-cites).

### 4. Reviews with detail

Ask patients and clients to mention the service and what it was like. "Dr. Lee explained my options and my implant was done in two visits" says more than five stars. Follow your field's rules on soliciting reviews; some restrict offering anything in return.

### 5. Mentions from trusted sources

Professional association directories, hospital affiliations, local press quotes, speaking pages, community sponsorships. [Brand mentions and AI search](/notes/brand-mentions-ai-search) covers how this adds up.

## The rules, briefly

I'm not a lawyer, and the rules vary by state and profession. Common restrictions include claims of specialization without certification, testimonials about outcomes, guarantees, and before-and-after images without disclaimers. Content written for AI search is still advertising. Have new pages reviewed the way you'd review an ad.

## A practice I've worked on

One pain clinic I do SEO for gets most of its clicks from phones, at an 11.26% click-through rate against about 2% on desktop, across hundreds of "near me" variations of its services. ## Next steps

Ask ChatGPT, Perplexity and Google three questions a new patient or client would ask, without your practice's name, and note who's recommended. Run each twice more in fresh sessions; answers vary a lot between runs. If a competitor is named consistently and you aren't, compare their profile, reviews and pages with yours.
