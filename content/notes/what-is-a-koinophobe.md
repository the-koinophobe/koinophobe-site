A koinophobe is a person with koinophobia: the fear that you've lived an ordinary life.

It's a newer word than it sounds, and it gets mixed up with a few others, so here's where it comes from, what it doesn't mean, and why it's on the front of my business.

## Where the word comes from

Koinophobia was coined by John Koenig for The Dictionary of Obscure Sorrows, his long-running project of invented words for feelings that didn't have a name yet. He released it as a short video in 2015 with the definition "the fear that you've lived an ordinary life."


The roots are Greek. Koinos means common or shared. It's the same root as Koine, the "common" Greek spoken across the ancient Mediterranean. Phobos means fear. Koinophobe is the person, the same way an arachnophobe is the person with arachnophobia.

It isn't a medical diagnosis and you won't find it in a clinical manual. It's a word for a feeling a lot of people recognize: scrolling through other people's lives at 1am and wondering whether yours will add up to anything.

## Koinophobia and koinoniphobia are different things

Search the word and Google will often hand you koinoniphobia instead. That one is a fear of rooms, or of rooms full of people, and Cleveland Clinic lists it as a specific phobia. Koinonia is Greek for fellowship or gathering, so the roots sit close together, but the meanings don't overlap.

Quick way to keep them apart:

| Word | What it means | Root |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Koinophobia | Fear of having lived an ordinary life | koinos, common |
| Koinophobe | A person who has koinophobia | koinos, common |
| Koinoniphobia | Fear of rooms or crowded rooms | koinonia, gathering |

## What it means for your site

The name fits the work too. Most small business websites are ordinary in the same handful of ways. A template homepage that says "quality service you can trust". One page trying to cover every service and every town. No tracking on the phone number, so nobody knows which calls came from Google. Google treats that kind of site the way you'd expect, and it ends up on page three.

What gets a site off page three is rarely clever. A page for each town you work in. Titles that say what you do and where. A Google Business Profile that agrees with your website. A way to count the calls. The [five case studies on the work page](/work) show what that looks like in Search Console numbers, including the parts that didn't move.
