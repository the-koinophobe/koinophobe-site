Two businesses. Same street, same service. Someone asks ChatGPT who to call, and it names one of them. The other owner never finds out the question was asked.

On November 10, OpenAI announced a way for businesses to claim a profile inside ChatGPT, in beta. Your details, your prices, and a count of how often ChatGPT mentions you. It sounds like the fix. The small print says otherwise: the agent you get to run is labelled, and kept apart from ChatGPT's own answers. Those answers come from somewhere else. Your website, your reviews, your listings, and what other people wrote about you. Call it your file. You didn't write most of it.

This note is the written version of Case File 001. It opens four real files, with the numbers straight from each business's Google Search Console. The businesses aren't named. If you want the broader checklist for getting named by assistants, it's in [how to get your business recommended by ChatGPT](/notes/get-business-recommended-by-chatgpt). This note is about the new profiles, and what actually decides whether you're named.

## The old file and the new reader

For about twenty years, getting found worked like a phone book with better lighting. Someone typed what they needed, Google showed ten links, they clicked one and called. The file Google kept on you was mostly your website.

Now something else reads that file first. Ask ChatGPT or Google's AI who to call and there's no list of ten. There's an answer, usually two or three names. The assistant has already read about you, and it decides before the customer does. So the question stops being "do I rank?" and becomes "what does my file say when I'm not in the room?"

## File 002: famous by name, invisible by need

A car window tint shop in Massachusetts. Good work, good reviews, people know it.

Of the 198 clicks Google reports for it, 166 came from people typing the shop's name. People who already knew it found it. For "window tinting near me", Google showed the shop 511 times, at an average position of 17. It got 3 clicks. Ceramic tint, car tint and tint shop searches look the same: shown hundreds of times, almost never chosen.

The file says who this shop is. It barely says what it does, or why someone new should pick it. And an assistant answering "who should I call?" is answering for exactly the people who don't know your name yet.

## File 003: the page nobody planned

A roofer in Florida. On paper a big file: about 184,000 times shown on Google in a year, at a 0.2% click-through rate.

For "roofing near me", shown 1,424 times, it got no clicks. For "roof repair near me", shown 1,767 times, it got 4. The visitors came from somewhere else: one article answering a question Florida homeowners keep asking, the 25% roof replacement rule. That article earned 75 clicks, more than five times the best service page on the site, which earned 14.

Nobody searches for a roofer. They search for their problem.

## File 004: answers, not adverts

A solar installer in Nigeria. In the week of September 1, 2026, its file was nearly empty: 66 appearances on Google.

The pages that started getting picked up weren't "best solar company". They were questions, in the customer's own words: can solar power an air conditioner, how many panels for a 5 kVA inverter, can solar run a fridge, a pressing iron, a borehole pump. And a page with the prices on it.

By the week of November 16 the site was shown 6,636 times in a week, with 60 clicks. The top page was the air conditioner one: 3,016 appearances and 14 clicks. It's three months of data and early. But look at what got picked up. Not claims. Answers.

## Back to the clinic

The case file opens with a therapy clinic in Florida that got 4 clicks from Google in a month, and 104 by April. In order, the work was: move the old site to the new one with every old address redirected, rebuild the site, give every treatment its own page with pages that answer what patients ask, the on-page basics, and tracking last. The full story is in [how a clinic went from 4 to 104 Google clicks a month](/notes/clinic-4-to-104-clicks).

Then over the summer its average position slid, from about 14 in May to about 31 in August, and came back to around 20 by the autumn. We don't know why, and we haven't guessed. Your file is never finished. Other people keep writing in it.

## Open your own file this week

No tools, no agency. Four steps:

1. **Ask the AI about yourself.** Open ChatGPT and ask who's the best at what you do, near you. Write down who it names and what it says about them.
2. **Claim what you can.** Your Google Business Profile, and now the ChatGPT Business Hub. Fill in every field.
3. **Answer the ten questions.** Write down the ten questions customers ask before they buy, and give each one a real answer on your site, in their words, not yours.
4. **Put your prices on the page.** Even a range. When someone asks what it costs, a file with no prices has nothing to say, and that makes you easier to pass over.

If you'd rather someone else did steps 2 to 4, that's what [the Google fix](/pricing#fix) covers.
